      Order of Convergence of Forward Euler and the Lax Equivalence Theorem#

      We’ve seen how to place bounds on the local truncation error \( \tau_n \)

      \[\begin{aligned} \text{Forward Euler: } \quad & \tau_n = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t) \\ \text{Backward Euler: } \quad & \tau_n = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t) \\ \text{Trapezoid method: } \quad & \tau_n = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2) \end{aligned}\]

      and observed empirically that forward Euler is first-order convergent. But what we really care about is the global error \( e_n \) . Ideally we want to show that if \( \tau_n = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^p) \) then \( e_n = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^p) \) . This is often much harder than computing the order of accuracy. In this lecture we prove it for forward Euler, then state a fundamental theorem of Lax that simplifies the analysis for more complex methods.

      Deriving the Order of Convergence of Forward Euler#

      Let’s derive the order of convergence for forward Euler. At the very first timestep:

      \[u_1 = u_0 + \Delta t \, f(t_0, u_0)\]

      Since \( u_0 = u(0) \) is exact, the truncation error \( \tau_1 = \dfrac{u(t_1) - u(t_0)}{\Delta t} - f(t_0, u(t_0)) = \dfrac{u(t_1) - u_1}{\Delta t} \) , so

      \[e_1 = u(t_1) - u_1 = \Delta t \, \tau_1\]

      The next estimate is

      \[\begin{aligned} u_2 &= u_1 + \Delta t \, f(t_1, u_1) \\ &= u(t_1) + \Delta t \, f(t_1, u(t_1)) + \left(u_1 - u(t_1)\right) + \Delta t \left( f(t_1, u_1) - f(t_1, u(t_1)) \right) \end{aligned}\]

      so

      \[e_2 = u(t_2) - u_2 = \Delta t \, \tau_2 + \Big( \Delta t \, \tau_1 + \Delta t \left( f(t_1, u(t_1)) - f(t_1, u_1) \right) \Big)\]

      Naively, this looks bad: we end up with \( N = T/\Delta t \) terms \( \Delta t \, \tau_n \) to add up, plus the propagated error from each step. That could kill a whole order of accuracy, or worse, if errors are amplified.

      In the general case, the true solution satisfies the scheme up to the truncation error, while the numerical solution satisfies it exactly

      \[\begin{aligned} u(t_n) - u(t_{n-1}) - \Delta t \, f(t_{n-1}, u(t_{n-1})) &= \Delta t \, \tau_n \\ u_n - u_{n-1} - \Delta t \, f(t_{n-1}, u_{n-1}) &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

      Rearrange and subtract to get a recurrence for the global error

      \[\overbrace{u(t_n) - u_n}^{e_n} = \overbrace{u(t_{n-1}) - u_{n-1}}^{e_{n-1}} + \Delta t \left( f(t_{n-1}, u(t_{n-1})) - f(t_{n-1}, u_{n-1}) \right) + \Delta t \, \tau_n\]

      If \( f \) is Lipschitz continuous in its second argument,

      \[\exists \, L > 0 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad |f(t, u) - f(t, v)| \leq L |u - v| \quad \forall \, t, u, v\]

      then we can bound the terms on the r.h.s. in absolute value

      \[\begin{aligned} |e_n| &\leq |e_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, L \, |u(t_{n-1}) - u_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, |\tau_n| \\ &= |e_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, L \, |e_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, |\tau_n| \\ |e_n| &\leq (1 + \Delta t \, L) |e_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, |\tau_n| \end{aligned}\]

      We call the \( (1 + \Delta t \, L) \) coefficient an amplification factor. It’s always \( > 1 \) in this case. Applying the bound again,

      \[|e_n| \leq (1 + \Delta t \, L)^2 |e_{n-2}| + (1 + \Delta t \, L) \Delta t \, |\tau_{n-1}| + \Delta t \, |\tau_n|\]

      and taking this all the way back to \( t = 0 \),

      \[|e_n| \leq (1 + \Delta t \, L)^n \overbrace{|e_0|}^{0} + \Delta t \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (1 + \Delta t \, L)^k \, |\tau_{n-k}|\]

      using \( e_0 = u(0) - u_0 = 0 \) . Now suppose that \( u^{\prime \prime}(t) \) is a bounded function, that is \( \displaystyle \sup_{0 \leq t \leq T} |u''(t)| \leq M \) for some \( M > 0 \). Then by the Lagrange (mean-value) form of the Taylor remainder, \( \tau_n = \frac{\Delta t}{2} u''(\xi_n) \) for some \( \xi_n \in [t_{n-1}, t_n] \) , so \( |\tau_n| \leq \dfrac{M}{2} \Delta t \) and

      \[\begin{aligned} |e_n| &\leq (1 + \Delta t \, L) |e_{n-1}| + \frac{M}{2} \Delta t^2 \\ &\leq \frac{M}{2} \Delta t^2 \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (1 + \Delta t \, L)^k \\ &= \frac{M}{2} \Delta t^2 \, \frac{(1 + \Delta t \, L)^n - 1}{\Delta t \, L} \end{aligned}\]

      using the geometric series \( \sum_{k=0}^{K} a r^k = a \frac{1 - r^{K+1}}{1 - r} \). Now we need to control the \( (1 + \Delta t \, L)^n \) . Realizing

      \[1 + a \leq e^a\]

      we get

      \[\begin{aligned} (1 + \Delta t \, L)^n &\leq e^{n \Delta t L} \leq e^{TL} \\ \to |e_n| &\leq \frac{M}{2} \frac{\left(e^{TL} - 1\right)}{L} \Delta t = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t) \end{aligned}\]

      So forward Euler is convergent of order one, under the assumptions we made along the way (\( f \) Lipschitz in \( u \), \( u^{\prime \prime} \) bounded). Note the constant grows like \( e^{TL} \): the bound is only useful on finite time intervals.

      Consistency + Stability = Convergence#

      The important intuition from the calculation above is that the global error ends up the same order as the local truncation error. The proof was specific to forward Euler, but a fundamental theorem of numerical analysis says this property holds for much larger families of algorithms. First a definition.

      Definition: 0-stability#

      Consider a numerical ODE solver with difference operator \( \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t} \) . The method is 0-stable if there exist constants \( C, h_0 > 0 \) , independent of \( \Delta t \), such that for all \( \Delta t \leq h_0 \) and any two sequences \( \{v_n\}_{n=0}^N \) and \( \{w_n\}_{n=0}^N \)

      \[|v_n - w_n| \leq C \left[ |v_0 - w_0| + \max_{1 \leq j \leq n} \left| \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[v_j] - \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[w_j] \right| \right]\]

      Think of \( v_n \) and \( w_n \) as two numerical solutions of the ODE. 0-stability says that, for small enough \( \Delta t \), the difference between them stays bounded by the difference in initial conditions plus the perturbations introduced through the difference operator. The “0” refers to requiring \( \Delta t \) sufficiently small: it’s a notion of stability in the limit \( \Delta t \to 0 \).

      This brings us to the Lax equivalence theorem, due to Peter Lax.

      Theorem: Lax Equivalence

      A numerical method that is consistent and 0-stable is also convergent. In fact, if the method is consistent of order \( p \), then it is also convergent of order \( p \).

      Proof

      Take \( v_n = u(t_n) \) (the true solution) and \( w_n = u_n \) (produced by our scheme). By definition \( \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[u(t_j)] = \tau_j \) and \( \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[u_j] = 0 \) , and \( e_0 = 0 \) . Substituting into the definition of 0-stability,

      \[|e_n| \leq C \left[ |e_0| + \max_{1 \leq j \leq n} \left| \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[u(t_j)] - \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t}[u_j] \right| \right] = C \max_{1 \leq j \leq n} |\tau_j| = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^p)\]

      The proof is simple, which means most of the work in showing the order of convergence goes into verifying the conditions. For any other method:

      1. Bound the local truncation error \( \tau_n \) (consistency)
      2. Prove the resulting difference operator \( \mathcal{N}_{\Delta t} \) is 0-stable

      Both steps lean heavily on Taylor expansions and additional smoothness assumptions, as in the forward Euler example. (Our forward Euler proof is essentially a hands-on version of this argument: the recurrence \( |e_n| \leq (1 + \Delta t L)|e_{n-1}| + \Delta t |\tau_n| \) sums to \( |e_n| \leq \frac{e^{TL} - 1}{L} \max_j |\tau_j| \) , so the stability constant is \( C = (e^{TL} - 1)/L \).)
